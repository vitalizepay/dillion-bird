Oman gets overlooked, and I have never fully understood why.

Clients arrive with the GCC mapped out in their heads before we have spoken. Dubai for the headquarters, Riyadh for the giga-project work, maybe Abu Dhabi if the sector fits. Oman comes up as an afterthought, if it comes up at all. Then we walk through the actual numbers and the room changes, because the case for Oman is stronger than its reputation suggests, and it has been getting stronger for several years while everyone was looking elsewhere.

## Why Oman, and Why Now

The short answer is that Oman has spent the last several years quietly removing the obstacles that used to make it awkward for foreign businesses, and the market has not fully repriced that yet.

The turning point was the Foreign Capital Investment Law under Royal Decree 50/2019, which scrapped the old requirement for an Omani shareholder across most commercial activities. Before that, doing business in Oman meant finding a local partner and building a structure around that constraint. Now, in the majority of sectors, you can own your Omani company outright. That single change reframed the entire market-entry conversation.

Around it sits Oman Vision 2040, the national diversification strategy steering capital away from hydrocarbons and toward logistics, manufacturing, tourism, renewables, fisheries and mining. This is not a slide deck. It shows up as real infrastructure: the Special Economic Zone at Duqm, the industrial and port complexes at Sohar and Salalah, and the free zone at Al Mazunah on the Yemeni border.

Then there is geography, which is Oman's genuinely underrated asset. Oman sits outside the Strait of Hormuz. For anyone whose business involves moving physical goods, that is not a trivia point, it is a supply chain risk consideration, and it is a large part of why Duqm and Salalah keep attracting logistics and industrial investment.

The last piece is quieter and harder to quantify. Oman is a calm place to do business. Regulatory change tends to be signalled well in advance rather than sprung. The personal income tax announcement is a good example: legislated in 2025, effective in 2028, giving everyone three years to plan. That predictability has real commercial value, and businesses that have been through abrupt regulatory shifts elsewhere in the region tend to appreciate it more than newcomers do.

## The Tax Position, Stated Plainly

Here is what you need to know, without the hedging.

**Corporate income tax is 15%** for most companies. Qualifying small and medium enterprises may pay **3%** where they meet the Oman Tax Authority's conditions, which is a meaningful difference for a business in its early years and worth structuring toward if you legitimately qualify.

**VAT is 5%** on most goods and services, in line with the GCC framework and among the lowest rates anywhere.

**There is no personal income tax in Oman today.** From **1 January 2028** that changes. Royal Decree No. 56/2025, issued on 22 June 2025, introduces a **5% tax on individual income above OMR 42,000 a year**, approximately USD 109,000. Oman is the first GCC country to legislate personal income tax, which drew a lot of regional attention when it was announced.

Two things are worth saying about that. First, the threshold is high and the rate is low, so for most employees it changes nothing. Second, and more importantly, it does not undermine the case for Oman. A 5% top-slice tax arriving in 2028, on income above roughly USD 109,000, in a country with 15% corporate tax and 5% VAT, still leaves Oman highly competitive globally. What it does mean is that senior executive packages structured today should be modelled with 2028 in mind rather than repriced in a hurry later.

## How Company Formation Actually Works

Registration runs through the **Ministry of Commerce, Industry and Investment Promotion**, known as MOCIIP, using its **Invest Easy** portal. The digital infrastructure here is better than most people expect, and better than several of Oman's neighbours.

The realistic sequence:

Reserve the trade name and confirm your commercial activity. This step matters more than it appears, because your activity code determines your foreign ownership position, your licensing requirements and sometimes your Omanisation obligations. Getting it wrong here creates work later.

Prepare and authenticate the constitutional documents. Corporate shareholders and overseas parent companies need attested paperwork, and this is consistently the step that takes longer than clients budget for.

Register with MOCIIP and obtain the commercial registration. Then register separately with the **Oman Tax Authority** for a Tax Identification Number. These are two distinct registrations, and assuming the first covers the second is a common early error.

Open a corporate bank account. Expect proper scrutiny. Omani banks apply serious compliance standards, and the file you present determines your timeline. Much of what we wrote about [preparing a UAE corporate banking application](/blog/uae-corporate-bank-account-guide) transfers directly to Oman: clarity on source of funds, a coherent explanation of the business model, and clean corporate documentation.

Then handle the operational layer: VAT registration if you cross the threshold, labour registrations, a physical address, and your Omanisation position. Omanisation is the national workforce policy requiring companies to employ a minimum proportion of Omani nationals, with quotas that vary by sector and company size. Treat it as a founding design constraint rather than a compliance problem to solve in year two. Businesses that build hiring plans around it from the start have a materially easier time than those that retrofit.

## Mainland or Free Zone

This decision drives everything downstream, so make it deliberately.

**A mainland company** registered with MOCIIP can trade freely throughout Oman and contract with government entities. For consultancies, professional services, retail, and anyone whose customers are Omani businesses or public bodies, this is almost always right.

**A free zone entity** in Duqm, Sohar, Salalah or Al Mazunah offers incentive packages built for manufacturing, logistics, processing and re-export. If your operation is capital-intensive and export-facing, the economics can be compelling.

The test I give clients is simple. Where are your customers, and where do your goods physically move? Answer that honestly and the structure usually picks itself. What does not work is choosing a free zone for the headline incentives and then discovering your actual revenue requires mainland trading rights. Restructuring afterwards is expensive and slow.

## The Mistakes We See Most

**Treating Oman like a smaller UAE.** The two markets share a region and very little else procedurally. Different regulator, different portal, different tax authority, different labour framework, different business culture. Assumptions carried over from a Dubai setup are the single biggest source of avoidable delay.

**Underestimating relationships.** Oman rewards patience and long-term thinking more than the faster-moving markets nearby. Deals move at the speed of trust. Firms that arrive expecting to transact in a fortnight generally leave disappointed, and often blame the market rather than their own approach.

**Getting the activity code wrong.** It is a dropdown on a form, and it determines your ownership position, your licensing and your compliance load. Fixing it later means amending your commercial registration, which is neither quick nor free.

**Leaving accounting until it is a problem.** Oman requires proper books and, depending on your structure and size, audited accounts. Clients who set up bookkeeping properly from month one never think about it again. Clients who do not spend their second year reconstructing their first.

**Ignoring 2028.** Personal income tax is legislated and dated. Executive packages agreed now should be modelled against it.

Closing a company in Dubai is an administrative process with a legal spine, and most of the expensive mistakes owners make come from treating it as though it were only the former. A trade licence is not a subscription you can simply stop paying. Until an entity is formally deregistered, it continues to exist, continues to accrue obligations, and continues to attach responsibility to the people who ran it.

This guide sets out how company liquidation and insolvency actually work in Dubai in 2026: which law now applies, the step-by-step process for mainland and free zone entities, what genuinely drives the timeline, the tax deregistration deadlines that catch people out, and where directors carry personal exposure.

## Liquidation and Insolvency Are Not the Same Thing

These two words get used interchangeably in conversation, and the confusion is costly.

**Liquidation** is a process. It is the orderly closure of a company: appointing a liquidator, notifying creditors, settling liabilities, obtaining clearances, and having the entity struck from the commercial register.

**Insolvency** is a financial condition. It means the company cannot pay its debts as they fall due, or its liabilities exceed its assets.

A solvent company can liquidate voluntarily. It pays what it owes, distributes whatever remains to shareholders, and closes. An insolvent company cannot do the same thing, because once there is not enough to go round, the law - not the shareholders - determines how the remaining value is distributed among creditors. Running an insolvent company through a standard voluntary closure, paying friendly creditors first and leaving others short, is precisely the behaviour that converts a corporate problem into a personal one.

The judgement call that matters most, and the one directors most often delay, is deciding which situation they are actually in.

## The Law That Applies in 2026

This is worth stating plainly, because a great deal of published material on UAE liquidation is out of date.

The governing federal legislation is now **Federal Decree-Law No. 51 of 2023 on Financial Restructuring and Bankruptcy**. It was published in October 2023 and came into force on **1 May 2024**, repealing Federal Decree-Law No. 9 of 2016, which is the law most older guides still cite.

Three changes matter commercially:

**A dedicated Bankruptcy Court.** The new law establishes a specialised court, supported by a dedicated Bankruptcy Unit, to oversee proceedings. Cases are handled by a forum built for them rather than the general civil docket.

**Preventive settlement replaces preventive composition.** The old preventive composition mechanism was so tightly conditioned that it went largely unused. It has been replaced by "preventive settlement", a more workable route that lets a company under pressure put a settlement or restructuring plan to its creditors while continuing to trade. For a business with a liquidity problem rather than a viability problem, this is the provision worth understanding before liquidation is even on the table.

**Broader management liability.** The scope of liability now reaches senior executive management, not only formally appointed directors. Actions - and equally, inaction - that contribute to the company's insolvency can attach to the people who were actually running the business.

Dubai's financial free zones sit outside this federal regime entirely. DIFC entities are governed by **DIFC Insolvency Law No. 1 of 2019** alongside the DIFC Companies Law, administered through the DIFC Courts. ADGM entities in Abu Dhabi fall under the **ADGM Insolvency Regulations 2022**. Both are common-law frameworks with their own procedures and their own courts. A DIFC winding-up is a different legal exercise from a mainland liquidation, and needs to be approached that way from the first day.

## Which Dubai Regime Governs Your Company

Before anything else, establish which authority owns your file. It determines the process, the paperwork and the timeline.

- **Mainland companies** licensed by Dubai's Department of Economy and Tourism (DET, formerly DED)
- **Free zone entities** in DMCC, JAFZA, DAFZA, Meydan, IFZA and the rest, each with its own registration authority and its own liquidation procedure layered on the federal framework
- **DIFC entities**, under DIFC Insolvency Law and the DIFC Courts

"Liquidating a Dubai company" is not one procedure. Assuming a DMCC process will mirror a DET process, or that either resembles a DIFC winding-up, is the most common structural error at the outset.

## The Dubai Mainland Liquidation Process, Step by Step

For a DET-licensed LLC, the sequence runs roughly as follows.

**1. Shareholder resolution.** The shareholders pass and notarise a resolution to dissolve the company and appoint a liquidator. For companies with corporate or foreign shareholders, this step alone can take time, since documents may need attestation.

**2. Appoint a registered liquidator.** The liquidator must be registered and issues a formal letter of acceptance. This letter is part of the filing bundle, not an afterthought.

**3. File with DET and obtain the liquidation certificate.** Once filed, the company's status changes to "Under Liquidation" on the commercial register. It still exists at this point. It is not closed.

**4. Publish the creditor notice.** The liquidator publishes notice in two local newspapers, one Arabic and one English. Creditors then have a **minimum of 45 days** from publication to submit claims. This period is fixed and cannot be compressed. It is the single largest immovable block in the timeline.

**5. Settle liabilities and cancel visas.** During the notice period, work through employee visa cancellation, end-of-service settlements, the establishment immigration card and labour file, supplier and landlord obligations, and any outstanding fines. Employee entitlements are not a line item to be negotiated down at this stage.

**6. Obtain clearance certificates.** Clearances are required from the relevant bodies, which typically include immigration and labour, utilities, telecoms, and where applicable customs and roads authorities. Each is a separate application on its own schedule.

**7. Close bank accounts and complete tax deregistration.** Corporate bank accounts must be formally closed and a bank closure letter obtained. Tax deregistration runs in parallel and is covered below.

**8. Liquidator's report and final deregistration.** The liquidator prepares the liquidation audit report, confirming assets, liabilities and shareholder equity at closure. This is a distinct document from your annual audit and covers the period from the last audited year end through to the liquidation date. It is a mandatory submission. On acceptance, DET cancels the trade licence and issues the deregistration certificate. Only now is the company closed.

## Free Zone Liquidation: Where It Differs

Free zone entities follow the same broad shape - resolution, liquidator, notice, clearances, audit report, deregistration - but the detail varies materially between authorities. Notice periods, the exact document set, the sequence in which clearances must be obtained, and whether the authority requires its own approved liquidator all differ from one free zone to the next.

Two practical points hold across nearly all of them. First, the liquidation audit report is required by free zone authorities just as it is by DET. Second, the free zone will not release final deregistration while any visa remains active on the establishment's immigration file. Confirm the current requirements with your specific authority rather than working from a general checklist, including one like this.

## What Actually Drives the Timeline

A Dubai mainland LLC liquidation typically runs **three to six months**. The 45-day notice period is only part of it. In practice, the schedule is set by whichever of the following is worst prepared:

- **Unfiled or incomplete accounts.** The liquidator cannot produce the audit report without them, and this is the most common cause of a stalled liquidation.
- **Active employee visas.** Every visa must be cancelled, and each has its own process and settlement.
- **Open tax positions.** Outstanding VAT returns or corporate tax filings must be brought current before deregistration is approved.
- **Bank accounts.** Formal closure and a closure letter, which banks do not always issue quickly.
- **Unresolved disputes.** Any live claim against the company will hold the process where it stands.

Companies that begin gathering documents at step one, rather than at step six, routinely close months sooner than those that do not.

## Tax Deregistration: The Deadline Most Owners Miss

This is where an otherwise clean liquidation turns into an avoidable penalty.

**Corporate tax.** You must notify the Federal Tax Authority and apply to deregister within **three months** of the cessation, dissolution or liquidation event. Miss it and the penalty is **AED 1,000**, followed by a further **AED 1,000 every month** on the same date, capped at **AED 10,000**. Applications go through the EmaraTax portal, and the FTA reviews within 30 business days.

**VAT.** A separate process with its own clock: generally **20 business days** from ceasing taxable supplies. It runs alongside corporate tax deregistration, not instead of it.

The trap is that the corporate tax three-month clock starts at cessation, while the liquidation itself may take six months to complete. Deregistration is not something to leave until the licence is cancelled. It has to be managed in parallel, from the moment the business stops trading.

## Where Directors Carry Personal Exposure

Limited liability is not unconditional, and Decree-Law 51 of 2023 widened the aperture to include senior executive management.

Exposure typically arises from a recognisable pattern: continuing to incur debt when the company was already unable to meet its obligations; paying selected creditors ahead of others once insolvency was apparent; disposing of assets at less than value in the run-up; and failing to act - failing to file, failing to escalate, failing to seek formal protection - when the financial position had clearly deteriorated.

The common thread is delay. The director who engages early, documents the reasoning behind decisions and uses the formal mechanisms available is in a substantially stronger position than one who waits and hopes for a turnaround. Where the business is under pressure but still fundamentally viable, preventive settlement exists precisely to preserve that value. Its usefulness depends entirely on being invoked in time.

## If the Company Is Insolvent Rather Than Simply Closing

Stop before starting a voluntary liquidation, and take three steps.

First, establish the position honestly, with a realistic assessment of assets, liabilities and forward cash flow rather than an optimistic one. Second, decide whether this is a liquidity problem or a viability problem, because that determines whether restructuring or closure is the right route. Third, take advice before making payments. Once insolvency is a live possibility, the order in which creditors are paid stops being a commercial choice and becomes a legal question.

## The Mistakes That Cost the Most

- **Letting the licence lapse instead of liquidating.** Fines accrue, the entity stays on the register, and the liabilities resurface at the worst possible moment - usually when applying for a new licence, visa or bank account.
- **Leaving tax deregistration until the end.** The corporate tax clock runs from cessation, not from licence cancellation.
- **Treating the liquidation audit report as the annual audit.** They are different documents with different scope.
- **Cancelling visas late.** No authority issues final deregistration while visas remain active.
- **Assuming free zone and mainland processes match.** They do not, and neither resembles DIFC.
- **Paying friendly creditors first when insolvent.** This is the single fastest route to personal liability.

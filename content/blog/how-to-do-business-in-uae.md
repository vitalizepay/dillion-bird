Setting up a company in the UAE is the straightforward part. The licence takes days, the structure is a decision you can be advised through, and there are hundreds of firms who will handle the paperwork.

What happens next is harder, and far less written about. I spend most of my time with businesses that are properly incorporated, correctly licensed and still not growing, because the strategy questions that actually determine success were never asked.

This guide covers those: how selling, hiring, pricing and getting paid actually work here, and the decisions that separate businesses that build something from businesses that hold a trade licence. If you are still at the setup stage, start with our [complete guide to company formation in Dubai](/blog/company-formation-in-dubai) and come back to this.

## The Market Is Not One Market

The first correction I make with almost every new client.

Dubai, Abu Dhabi and the northern emirates behave differently. Abu Dhabi is more government and energy weighted, with longer procurement cycles and a preference for established counterparties. Dubai is faster, more international, more trade and services driven. Sharjah and the northern emirates compete harder on cost.

Beyond geography, the market is segmented by nationality, sector and price point in ways that surprise people. The international consultancy in DIFC and the family trading business in Deira are not competing for the same clients, do not buy the same way, and do not respond to the same positioning.

A proposition built for "the UAE market" generally appeals to nobody in particular. Pick a segment, learn how it actually buys, and build for that.

## Deals Move at the Speed of Trust

This is the adjustment foreign businesses find hardest, and it is where most underestimate their runway.

Business here runs on relationships to a degree that catches people out. A technically superior proposal from an unknown supplier frequently loses to an adequate one from a known quantity. That is not inefficiency; it is a rational response to a market with a lot of transient participants.

Practically, it means your sales cycle is longer than your business plan assumes. B2B deals, anything touching government, and work with large family groups routinely take several months. Decisions involve more people than the org chart suggests. Meetings that feel like conclusions are often just stages.

Three things follow. Plan cash flow against a long cycle and treat anything faster as upside. Invest in presence before you need it, because relationships built when you want nothing are worth more than relationships built during a pitch. And do not mistake politeness for commitment; a warm meeting is not a signed contract, and reading it as one is how forecasts go wrong.

## Pricing: Position Before You Discount

The UAE has room at the premium end and brutal competition at the commodity end, with less in between than you might expect.

Decide which you are. Businesses that try to compete on price against operators with lower cost bases usually lose, and those that price at a premium without a defensible reason struggle to explain themselves. The question worth answering is why someone pays you more, and if the honest answer is that they would not, your problem is the proposition rather than the pricing.

One specific warning. Discounting to win a first client here is common and often sensible, but the price you set becomes the reference point for that relationship and for anyone they refer you to. Discount with a stated reason and a stated end, or do not discount at all.

## Where Customers Actually Come From

Marketing plans written elsewhere travel badly here, because the channels that work are weighted differently.

**Referral and introduction** does more heavy lifting than in most markets, which follows from the relationship point above. A warm introduction from someone credible compresses a nine-month cycle into weeks. This is the single highest-return channel and the one most foreign businesses neglect because it is slow to build and impossible to buy.

**In-person presence** still matters. Sector events, chambers of commerce, industry associations and the free zone communities themselves generate real pipeline, partly because they are where introductions originate. A founder who attends nothing in their first year is building on a narrower base than they realise.

**LinkedIn** works well for B2B here, better than in many markets, because the professional population is large, international and active. It is also where people check you exist before responding to an approach. A thin profile and an empty company page cost you credibility you did not know you were spending.

**Search** matters for considered purchases and for anything where a buyer is actively looking: professional services, formation, technology, logistics. If your customers research before they call, being absent from search means being absent from the shortlist.

**Cold outreach** has the lowest yield of the five and is not pointless, but it works best as a way to earn a first meeting that then runs on relationship rules, rather than as a transactional channel.

The practical implication: budget for presence and patience, not just advertising. Businesses that spend heavily on paid acquisition while investing nothing in relationships tend to find their cost per acquisition stays stubbornly high.

## Partners and Distributors: Useful and Risky

For many foreign businesses the fastest route into the market is through a local partner, distributor or agent who already holds the relationships.

Done well, this is genuinely the shortcut it appears to be. Done badly, it is the most common way foreign companies lose control of their own market.

The risks worth planning around. An exclusive arrangement with an underperforming partner can lock you out of your own market for the term of the agreement. A partner who holds all the customer relationships holds your business. And commercial agency arrangements in particular carry legal consequences that are hard to unwind, so the structure needs proper advice before signature rather than after the relationship sours.

Practical guardrails: define performance expectations explicitly, keep exclusivity short or conditional on volume, maintain some direct customer contact of your own, and get the agreement reviewed by someone who knows the local framework. A partner who objects to all four is telling you something useful.

## Getting Paid Is a Strategy Problem

Dinesh, my fellow Managing Partner, handles most of our banking and finance work, and this is the thing he raises with clients more than any other.

Stated payment terms and actual payment behaviour frequently diverge, particularly in contracting, trading and work involving larger corporates. A business can be profitable on paper and genuinely unable to pay salaries, because the revenue exists as a receivable rather than cash.

Manage it structurally rather than hopefully:

- Stage payments against milestones rather than invoicing on completion
- Take deposits where your market allows it
- Invoice the day the work is done, not at month end
- Chase early and without embarrassment, because the businesses that get paid first are the ones that ask first
- Treat receivables ageing as a weekly number, not a monthly report

Our [guide to UAE corporate banking](/blog/uae-corporate-bank-account-guide) covers the facility side, and the [bookkeeping, tax and audit calendar](/blog/dubai-bookkeeping-tax-audit-compliance-calendar) covers the compliance obligations that sit alongside.

## Hiring: Know the Rules Before You Build the Plan

Employment is governed by **Federal Decree-Law No. 33 of 2021**, and several provisions shape your operating model directly.

**Probation cannot exceed six months** and cannot be extended. During probation an employer must give **14 days written notice** to terminate. An employee leaving the UAE gives 14 days; one moving to another UAE employer gives 30.

Fixed-term contracts, end-of-service entitlements and the Wage Protection System all affect your real cost per head. Model them before you commit to a hiring plan, because the headline salary is not the cost.

### Emiratisation

If you are a mainland company heading toward 50 or more skilled employees, this belongs in your plan now rather than later.

Mainland companies with **50 or more skilled employees** must reach **10% Emirati representation in skilled roles by 31 December 2026**, with the target rising **1% every six months**. A skilled role broadly means a position requiring a post-secondary qualification or recognised professional skill level, and the target is calculated against skilled roles rather than total headcount.

The cost of non-compliance is specific: a monthly contribution of **AED 9,000 per unfilled position**, which is **AED 108,000 a year** for each one.

Two unfilled positions is over AED 200,000 annually, which is material for a mid-sized business. Companies that build Emirati hiring into their workforce plan early manage it comfortably. Those that discover it at the threshold tend to pay while they scramble.

## The Operating Rhythm

Small things that affect execution and rarely appear in strategy documents.

The working week runs Monday to Friday, with Friday often shortened. Ramadan changes working hours, meeting availability and consumer behaviour significantly for a month, and the surrounding weeks are quieter than normal. Summer sees a meaningful share of decision-makers travel, and July and August are poor months to launch anything requiring senior attention.

Plan your year around this rather than being surprised by it. A product launch scheduled for mid-Ramadan or early August is a launch into an empty room.

## Documentation Discipline

A cultural note that is genuinely commercial.

Business here is relationship-led, which sometimes leads foreign operators to assume it is informal. It is not. Contracts matter, documentation matters, and the gap between a warm relationship and an enforceable agreement is where disputes live.

Write things down. Confirm verbal agreements by email. Keep records of variations, approvals and scope changes. The relationship gets you the work; the paperwork is what you rely on if something goes wrong, and relying on goodwill at that point is an expensive lesson.

## English Is Enough, Mostly

You do not need Arabic to build a business here, and most commercial activity runs in English.

Arabic matters for certain government interactions, official documentation and some client relationships. Budget for translation and attestation rather than fluency. What does matter, and is noticed, is cultural engagement: understanding the calendar, the courtesies, and the fact that relationship-building is not a preliminary to business but part of it.

## Expanding Across the GCC

The most common timing mistake I see: businesses expand regionally when UAE growth stalls, hoping a new market will fix the problem.

It rarely does. If the proposition is not working here, it is unlikely to work in Riyadh with an added layer of regulatory complexity and a team you cannot supervise.

Expand when the UAE operation is genuinely working and you have capacity to run two things properly. And treat each market as a new entry, not an extension: Saudi Arabia, Oman, Qatar, Kuwait and Bahrain each have their own regulators, ownership rules, customer expectations and pace. ## The Mistakes That Cost Most

**Treating the UAE as one market.** Covered above, and the root of most positioning failures.

**Underestimating the sales cycle.** The single most common cause of businesses running out of runway while their pipeline still looks healthy.

**Confusing activity with traction.** Meetings, events and LinkedIn presence are not revenue. Measure signed contracts and cash collected.

**Hiring ahead of revenue.** The visa and end-of-service structure makes headcount harder to reverse than in many markets.

**Leaving Emiratisation until the threshold.** It is a planning exercise with a known cost, not a surprise.

**Ignoring receivables.** Profitable businesses fail on cash, not on margin.

**Building the wrong structure for the business you are becoming.** The commonest version is a free zone licence for a business whose customers turn out to be UAE-based. Our [UAE business consultant guide](/blog/uae-business-consultant-praveen) covers the structural decision in detail.

**Expanding to escape.** Regional expansion amplifies whatever you already have, including the problems.

## A Realistic First Twelve Months

A sequence that works more often than the one most businesses attempt.

**Months 1 to 2: get operational properly.** Licence, bank account, accounting system from the first transaction, and tax registration. Resist the temptation to treat finance as something to sort out later; reconstructing a year of records is the most avoidable cost in this market.

**Months 1 to 3: choose a segment and test the proposition.** Not the whole UAE. One segment, defined tightly enough that you can name twenty target customers. Then talk to them. The purpose of this phase is learning whether your assumption about demand survives contact with buyers, which is cheaper to discover now than after you have hired.

**Months 2 to 6: build presence before you need it.** Events, associations, introductions, a credible digital footprint. This feels unproductive because it produces no immediate revenue, and it is the phase that determines what months seven to twelve look like.

**Months 4 to 9: first contracts, and learn your real cycle.** Track how long deals actually take from first contact to cash received, not to signature. That number is your planning input for everything afterwards, and it is almost always longer than the plan assumed.

**Months 6 to 12: hire against revenue, not hope.** Headcount is harder to reverse here than in many markets. If you are approaching 50 skilled employees, Emiratisation planning starts now rather than at the threshold.

**Month 12: review the structure against reality.** By now you know who actually buys from you. If your customers turned out to be UAE-based and you hold a free zone licence, this is the moment to address it, while it is still a correction rather than a rebuild.

## What Good Looks Like After Year One

The businesses that establish themselves here tend to share a pattern.

They picked a segment and got specific about it. They invested in relationships before needing them. They priced deliberately and defended it. They built financial discipline early, so they always knew their cash position. They planned headcount against regulation rather than around it. And they stayed long enough for the relationship-driven part of the market to start working in their favour, which generally takes longer than year one.

None of that is exotic. It is ordinary commercial discipline applied to a market with its own rhythm, and the main reason it fails is impatience.

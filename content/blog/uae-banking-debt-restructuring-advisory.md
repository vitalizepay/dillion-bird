Most of the conversations I have about banking start too late.

By the time an owner calls, the facility is already under strain, a payment has been missed, or an application has been declined without a clear explanation. The work is still worth doing at that point. But the range of options has narrowed considerably compared with where it stood three months earlier.

This sets out how I think about banking relationships, debt restructuring and financial distress for businesses in the UAE, and points to the detailed guides I have written on each.

## Banking Is a Strategic Relationship, Not an Administrative One

The most consistent error I see is treating the bank as a utility: somewhere money sits, approached only when something is needed.

A bank sees your business differently from how you see it. You look at revenue, customers, contracts and growth. The credit and compliance teams are looking at cash flow, repayment capacity, ownership, transaction patterns, source of funds and whether the business is sustainable. Both views are legitimate. They are not the same view, and the gap between them explains most declined applications.

Understanding that difference changes how you prepare. Our guide to [the UAE banking system](/blog/uae-banking-system-explained) sets out what the sector actually offers a business, and [opening and keeping a UAE corporate bank account](/blog/uae-corporate-bank-account-guide) covers what banks assess and why applications stall.

## Revenue Is Not Cash

If I could correct one habit in owner-managed businesses, it would be this one.

A company can report AED 20 million in annual revenue and be unable to meet payroll, because customers settle at 90 or 120 days while salaries, rent, suppliers and loan repayments fall due now. Profitable on paper, illiquid in practice.

Any business under pressure should be managed on cash rather than on the profit and loss. That means receivables ageing reviewed weekly rather than monthly, a rolling thirteen-week forecast, and a clear view of which obligations are genuinely critical. A business that can see thirteen weeks ahead is in a materially different position from one that discovers a problem when the balance is already too low to act on.

## Preparation Is What Changes the Outcome

Nobody can promise a lender's decision, and anyone who does should be treated with suspicion. What preparation changes is the quality of what the bank is deciding on.

Compare two approaches to the same situation.

The first: "We have a problem and we need more time."

The second: "Here is the current position, here is what caused the pressure, here is our twelve-month cash-flow forecast, and here is the restructuring proposal we believe restores stability."

Same company, same numbers, very different conversations. The second arrives with updated financials, debt schedules, receivables and payables ageing, recovery assumptions and proposed terms. It gives a credit committee something to work with.

That is the substance of banking advisory as I practise it. Not influence, which does not exist and should not be sold. Preparation.

## Restructuring or Insolvency: The Judgement That Matters Most

When pressure becomes serious, one question determines everything that follows.

Is this a **liquidity problem** in a fundamentally sound business, where the model works and the capital structure is wrong? That is a restructuring conversation: extended tenors, revised covenants, refinancing, consolidation, improved working capital, possibly new investment.

Or is it a **viability problem**, where the business no longer generates enough to service its obligations under any realistic scenario? That is a different conversation, and delaying it converts a manageable wind-down into a contested one.

Owners understandably resist the second answer. But the frameworks available in the UAE are considerably more useful to a business that engages early. Our guide to [UAE bankruptcy and insolvency](/blog/uae-bankruptcy-insolvency-restructuring-guide) covers both the corporate and personal frameworks, and the warning signs worth acting on.

## Where Closure Is the Right Answer

Not every business can or should be rescued, and recognising that early is competent management rather than failure.

What matters then is that it is orderly. A company should never simply stop trading, abandon the premises and walk away. Employees, creditors, suppliers, landlords and regulators all hold positions that need dealing with, and walking away creates personal exposure that a proper process does not.

## What I Would Ask You First

If you are weighing a financing decision, a restructuring, or a bank conversation you are not looking forward to, these are the questions I would start with.

1. What does your cash position look like over the next thirteen weeks?
2. Are your accounts current enough to answer that confidently?
3. How much debt does the business carry, and on what terms?
4. Are your customers paying within agreed terms?
5. Is the business profitable before financing costs?
6. Have you spoken to your lender yet, and how did it go?
7. If restructuring is not viable, what is the orderly alternative?

If those answers are difficult to produce, that is itself the finding, and the first piece of work.

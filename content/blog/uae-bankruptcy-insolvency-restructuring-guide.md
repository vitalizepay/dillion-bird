There is a belief among business owners that financial difficulty means the end of a company.

It does not.

A business can face declining cash flow, rising debt, creditor pressure, difficulty meeting bank obligations, even a period of genuine insolvency, without that being the end of the story. The question that matters is not whether a business is under financial pressure. Most businesses are, at some point. The question is what the owner does when the first warning signs appear.

In my experience, one principle holds almost without exception. Financial problems rarely appear overnight. The consequences arrive suddenly, but the warning signs were usually visible months earlier to anyone willing to look at them properly.

That is why these things are worth understanding before you need them.

## What Insolvency Actually Means

Insolvency is a situation where a business is having serious difficulty meeting its financial obligations. It can arise because there is not enough cash to pay debts as they fall due, or because the overall financial position has deteriorated to the point where liabilities are a genuine concern.

But insolvency is not the same as failure, and a company can be profitable on paper while being unable to pay anyone.

Consider a business with AED 20 million in annual revenue. Its customers owe it AED 5 million, and they are taking 120 days to pay. Meanwhile salaries, rent, suppliers and loan repayments all fall due this month. The revenue figure looks healthy. The bank balance tells a completely different story.

This is why I push owners to stop managing on revenue when pressure starts.

Revenue tells you how much you sold. Cash flow tells you whether you survive the quarter.

## Three Words That Are Not Interchangeable

These terms get used as though they mean the same thing, and the confusion leads people to the wrong conclusion.

**Insolvency** is a financial condition. The business cannot meet its obligations, or is heading that way. It is a signal that something has to change.

**Bankruptcy** is a legal framework and process for dealing with that condition. It includes mechanisms intended to rescue a business, not only to end it.

**Liquidation** is winding the company up: realising assets, settling liabilities in the order the law requires, and bringing the company's existence to an end.

Liquidation is not the automatic first step when a company hits difficulty. Often the better question is whether the business can be stabilised or restructured before liquidation becomes necessary at all.

## The Framework That Applies in the UAE

The governing law is **Federal Decree-Law No. 51 of 2023 on Financial Restructuring and Bankruptcy**, which came into force on **1 May 2024** and repealed the previous Federal Decree-Law No. 9 of 2016. A great deal of material still circulating online refers to the repealed law, which is worth knowing when you are researching your own position.

Its **executive regulations were issued as Cabinet Resolution No. 94 of 2024**, published on 16 September 2024. They established a bankruptcy register, designated the Central Bank and the Securities and Commodities Authority as supervisory entities, and set the debt thresholds for initiating proceedings.

What matters commercially is that the framework is not simply a mechanism for closing companies. It provides for consensual out-of-court restructuring, preventive settlement, formal financial restructuring, and bankruptcy and liquidation where nothing else is viable.

Business owners often believe the only two options are pay everything now or close the company. The reality is more nuanced than that, and the mechanisms that sit between those two poles are the ones most worth understanding early.

## Know Which Framework Applies to You

This is the step people skip, and it undermines everything that follows.

The federal framework applies to companies governed by the Commercial Companies Law, to natural persons with the capacity of a trader, and to licensed civil companies carrying on professional activities. It does not apply to natural persons generally.

Several categories sit outside it or are treated differently: certain government-owned entities, banks and financial institutions regulated by the Central Bank, insurance companies, and free zone entities operating under their own insolvency regimes. DIFC and ADGM entities in particular have separate, common-law based regimes administered through their own courts.

So before reading any general guidance, including this article, establish which regime actually governs your entity. Advice built on the wrong framework is worse than no advice.

## The Warning Signs That Get Ignored

The most common mistake I see is not bad strategy. It is delay.

Owners tell themselves the customer will pay, the bank will extend, the next project will fix it. Sometimes that is true. But every month of waiting tends to remove options rather than create them.

These are the signals that deserve attention the week you notice them:

**Persistent cash shortages.** If the company repeatedly cannot cover basic operating costs, that needs diagnosing, not repeatedly funding.

**Borrowing to service borrowing.** Debt supporting growth is healthy. Debt covering previous debt is a different situation entirely.

**Suppliers tightening terms.** When suppliers start demanding cash up front, they are telling you something about their confidence in your position. Listen to it.

**Difficulty meeting bank obligations.** Missed repayments, covenant pressure or repeated extension requests are not administrative inconveniences.

**Heavy customer concentration.** If one or two customers carry most of your revenue, losing a single contract becomes a liquidity crisis rather than a setback.

**Margins falling while revenue rises.** Growth can disguise deterioration. A business can be getting bigger and weaker simultaneously.

**Unresolved statutory obligations.** Outstanding regulatory and compliance matters compound quietly and surface at the worst moment.

**Continuous personal funding.** There is a difference between a founder bridging a gap once and a business that now depends on its owner's personal finances to operate.

**No reliable financial reporting.** If management cannot state the current cash position, receivables, payables and upcoming obligations, every decision after that is guesswork.

**Silence.** The most serious sign of all is when management stops talking to banks, suppliers, shareholders and advisors because the situation feels overwhelming. That is precisely the point at which the conversations matter most.

## The First Step Is Assessment, Not Panic

Before any major decision, establish the actual position. That means knowing: cash available, receivables and their real ageing, payables, bank facilities and repayment schedules, supplier and employee obligations, tax and regulatory exposure, fixed and variable costs, assets, existing security and guarantees, contractual commitments, and realistic expected revenue.

Not estimates. Numbers.

It sounds obvious. In practice, a significant proportion of businesses under pressure cannot produce this quickly, and that inability is itself part of the problem. Decisions get made on instinct because the information to make them properly does not exist.

## Restructuring Is Often the Better Answer

A company in difficulty should not assume liquidation is the destination.

If the underlying business is viable, restructuring frequently preserves more value. A company may have a solid customer base, real intellectual property, experienced staff and a decent market position, alongside too much short-term debt. In that situation the business is not the problem. The capital structure is.

Options can include renegotiating repayment schedules, refinancing, extending loan tenors, tightening working capital, negotiating with suppliers, reviewing the cost base, selling non-core assets, restructuring business units, improving collections, or bringing in new investment.

Which of these applies depends entirely on the facts. But the principle is simple: if the business is fundamentally sound, the objective is to preserve value rather than to unwind it.

## How to Talk to Your Bank

A business under pressure should not treat its bank as an adversary. Banks are managing risk. They want to know whether there is a credible plan, whether the business can recover, and whether the structure is sustainable.

Which means preparation changes the outcome.

Compare two approaches. The first: "We have a problem and we need more time." The second: "Here is the current position, here is what caused the pressure, here is our twelve-month cash-flow forecast, and here is the restructuring proposal we believe restores stability."

Same company. Very different conversations, and usually very different results.

Bring updated financials, cash-flow forecasts, debt schedules, receivables and payables ageing, your recovery assumptions and your proposed terms. ## When Liquidation Is the Right Decision

Not every business can or should be rescued, and recognising that early is a form of good management rather than a failure of it.

Liquidation becomes appropriate when the underlying business is no longer commercially viable, when liabilities cannot realistically be serviced under any scenario, when there is no credible restructuring path, or when shareholders no longer wish to continue.

The important word is orderly.

A company should never simply stop operating, abandon the premises, close the bank account and walk away. There are employees, creditors, suppliers, customers, regulators and lenders whose positions have to be dealt with, and walking away creates personal exposure that a properly run wind-down does not.

Depending on the entity and jurisdiction, an orderly closure involves regulatory filings, creditor notice, asset realisation, settlement of liabilities, clearances and formal deregistration. ## Do Not Move the Assets

This deserves its own warning.

When a company is under serious financial pressure, its assets and liabilities become part of a process governed by law and by creditors' rights. Cash, equipment, vehicles, stock, property, receivables, investments, intellectual property and contractual rights are all in scope.

Transferring, selling or disposing of company assets while the company is distressed can have legal consequences, particularly where it advantages one party over others. Paying a friendly creditor ahead of the rest, or moving an asset out of the company, is the fastest route to turning a corporate problem into a personal one.

Take advice before doing anything significant with company assets. Not after.

## Directors Should Act Deliberately

Financial distress puts real pressure on the people running a business, and it is natural to want to protect something you spent years building. But emotional decisions tend to make a difficult situation worse.

Under pressure, management should hold to facts, documentation, communication, professional advice and a realistic plan.

Keep proper records of financial decisions, board discussions, creditor communications and restructuring efforts. This is not administrative box-ticking. Good documentation demonstrates that management recognised the situation and acted deliberately, and that record matters if anyone later examines how the company was run during the period.

## The Thirteen-Week Forecast

The single most useful tool available to a business under pressure is also one of the simplest: a realistic rolling cash-flow forecast, thirteen weeks out.

It should answer: what cash is available today, what is expected in and when precisely will it arrive, what is due and which of those payments are genuinely critical, which liabilities could be renegotiated, and what happens if the money you are counting on arrives late.

Then stress it. What happens if revenue falls 10%, 20%, 30%?

A business that can see thirteen weeks ahead is in a materially different position from one that discovers a crisis when the balance is already too low to act on.

## Ten Questions Worth Answering Honestly

1. Do we know exactly how much debt the company carries?
2. Do we have an accurate thirteen-week cash-flow forecast?
3. Which creditors must be paid first, and on what basis?
4. Are customers paying within agreed terms?
5. Are our bank facilities still appropriate for the business we now run?
6. Is the underlying business profitable before financing costs?
7. Can the business survive a further fall in revenue?
8. Have we spoken to our lenders early enough?
9. Is restructuring realistically possible?
10. If it is not, what is the most orderly way to exit?

If those cannot be answered confidently, that is the finding, and it is worth acting on.

## Do Not Wait for the Crisis

This is the part I would most want a business owner to take away.

There is a real difference between arriving at a conversation saying "we are beginning to feel financial pressure" and arriving saying "we have missed several payments, creditors are demanding settlement, the bank is taking action and there is no cash left."

The first has options. The second may have very few.

Early intervention buys the thing that matters most in distress, which is time: time to understand the position, negotiate with stakeholders, test whether restructuring works, and decide on facts rather than under pressure.

Businesses go through cycles. Some grow quickly, some meet disruption they did not cause, some take on debt that made sense at the time, some are let down by customers who do not pay. Financial difficulty is serious, but it is a business problem requiring structured decisions. It is not a personal verdict on the person who built the company.

The right answer might be restructuring, refinancing, negotiation or recovery. Sometimes it is an orderly liquidation. What matters is understanding the options while you still have them.

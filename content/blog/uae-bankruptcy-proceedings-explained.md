Most writing about UAE bankruptcy treats it as a single event: a company runs out of money and the law steps in. The framework is more useful than that, because two of its three main routes are designed to keep a business trading rather than wind it up.

This piece explains the procedures themselves: what each one is for, the legal tests that trigger them, and the deadlines worth knowing before you need them. For the practical side of managing financial pressure, warning signs and talking to lenders, see our [guide to UAE bankruptcy and insolvency for business owners and individuals](/blog/uae-bankruptcy-insolvency-restructuring-guide).

## The Law That Applies

The governing legislation is **Federal Decree-Law No. 51 of 2023**, the Financial and Bankruptcy Law, in force since **1 May 2024**. It replaced Federal Decree-Law No. 9 of 2016, which a good deal of material online still cites. Its Executive Regulations were issued as **Cabinet Resolution No. 94 of 2024**.

The framework establishes preventive settlement, restructuring and bankruptcy, supported by a dedicated bankruptcy court and administration. The design intent is balance: giving a distressed debtor structured routes to recover while giving creditors an orderly process for dealing with claims.

## Cessation of Payment: A Narrower Test Than People Expect

This is the concept that catches directors out, because it is not a test of whether your company is solvent.

The law defines cessation of payment as failure to pay a debt that is due once **10 days have passed** beyond the deadline specified in the relevant notice. It applies **even where the debtor's assets are valuable enough to cover the debt**, and even where the debt is secured by sufficient collateral.

Read that again if your instinct is "we have assets, we are fine". Asset cover does not prevent the test being met. A business sitting on property or receivables while missing a payment deadline can be in cessation of payment as a matter of law.

## The 60-Day Window

Once cessation of payment occurs, or once the debtor becomes aware of information confirming it will be unable to pay debts as they fall due, there is a window to act.

The debtor may initiate preventive settlement, restructuring or bankruptcy proceedings by applying to the Bankruptcy Administration **within 60 days**.

Sixty days is not long when a company is simultaneously trying to collect receivables, negotiate with a bank and keep operating. It is the single strongest practical reason to get the position assessed early rather than waiting to see whether the next quarter recovers. Directors who start the analysis when pressure first appears have options inside that window. Those who start when creditors are already escalating often do not.

## Three Routes, Not One

### Preventive Settlement

The earliest and lightest-touch route. It is intended to let a financially distressed debtor keep running its business while reaching an arrangement with creditors.

Under this procedure the debtor generally continues managing its own business and assets in the ordinary course, implementing a proposed settlement approved and supervised through the applicable process. The aim is to resolve difficulties before the position becomes irreversible.

This replaced the old preventive composition mechanism, which was conditioned so tightly that it went largely unused. The current version is meant to be workable.

### Restructuring

The more formal rescue mechanism, for a business that remains capable of continuing but needs its obligations reorganised through a plan.

Applications may be made by the debtor and, subject to the law's requirements, by creditors or the relevant regulatory authority. The central question is whether the underlying business can keep going. Where it can, restructuring offers a path to reorganise liabilities rather than move straight to liquidation.

In commercial terms the distinction worth holding onto is this: if the business model still works and the problem is the capital structure, restructuring is the conversation. If the business itself no longer generates enough to service its obligations under any realistic scenario, it is not.

### Bankruptcy

Relevant where the business is no longer viable. Broadly, the conditions for opening proceedings run together: the debtor has stopped paying its debts, there is a deficiency in its financial position, and the business is not capable of continuing.

That third limb does the real work. It is what separates genuine bankruptcy from ordinary short-term cash-flow pressure, and it is why a company waiting on slow-paying customers is usually in a restructuring discussion rather than a bankruptcy one.

## What Changes When Proceedings Open

Proceedings are a formal legal process, not an announcement. The framework provides for bankruptcy courts and administration mechanisms to receive applications, verify documentation, notify interested parties and administer the procedure.

One consequence deserves flagging because it materially changes the arithmetic: the law provides that **the maturity dates of monetary debts can be accelerated** once bankruptcy proceedings are opened, subject to its provisions. Obligations a company expected to fall due over several years can become payable. Any decision to enter proceedings needs that modelled beforehand, not discovered afterwards.

## Where Creditors Stand

The framework is not only a shield for distressed debtors. It also sets out how creditors are dealt with.

Once a company cannot meet all its obligations, creditors cannot simply continue acting entirely independently of the process. The law provides an organised route for identifying claims, dealing with the debtor's assets and distributing proceeds according to the applicable rules and priorities.

For businesses on the creditor side, the practical implication is unglamorous but real: document your receivables properly, monitor ageing, and act early. A well-evidenced claim is in a materially better position than one assembled under pressure after proceedings open.

## Director Conduct Is Part of the Framework

The law contains provisions dealing with conduct that contributes to a company's financial deterioration. Among the behaviours it addresses: transactions designed to prejudice creditors, disposing of assets for less than adequate consideration, and paying selected creditors with the intention of harming others.

The practical rule follows directly. Once serious financial distress is a live possibility, take advice before doing anything significant with company assets or creditor payments. The order in which creditors get paid stops being a commercial choice and becomes a legal question, and that is the point at which a corporate problem most often becomes a personal one.

## Why Accurate Books Matter More Under Pressure

Every route above depends on being able to show your actual position, and quickly.

A company in difficulty needs to state, with confidence: cash genuinely available, receivables and their real ageing, payables, outstanding loans and guarantees, asset position and what is secured against it, and a forward cash-flow forecast.

In practice a significant proportion of distressed businesses cannot produce this at speed, and that inability is itself part of the problem. Decisions get made on instinct because the information to make them properly does not exist, and the 60-day window closes while the accounts are being reconstructed.

Good bookkeeping is not a compliance chore in this context. It is what determines whether you can use the options the law gives you.

## What to Do If Pressure Is Building

1. Produce an up-to-date statement of assets, liabilities, receivables, payables and cash
2. Build a realistic forward cash-flow forecast
3. Identify which creditors are critical and which are not
4. Review loan agreements, guarantees, leases and major supplier contracts
5. Take legal and financial advice before making significant decisions
6. Consider the rescue routes early, while the business is still viable enough to use them

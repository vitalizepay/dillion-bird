The question I am asked most often is some version of "how much does it cost to set up a company in Dubai?"

It is a reasonable question and I understand why it comes first. It is also, almost always, the wrong place to start. I have seen businesses save fifteen thousand dirhams on formation and then spend considerably more than that unwinding a structure that could not open a bank account, could not invoice the customers it actually had, or could not hire the people it needed.

The more useful question is quieter: what am I actually trying to build, and what structure supports that?

I am a Managing Partner at Dillon & Bird, a UAE consulting firm working across strategy, corporate services, finance and technology. This is the guidance I would give you across a table, written down.

## What a UAE Business Consultant Actually Does

There is a distinction worth being precise about, because the market blurs it.

A **company formation agent** answers "how do I register this company." They know the portals, the forms and the approvals, and a good one moves quickly. If your situation is simple and you already know what you want, that may be all you need, and I would tell you so.

A **business consultant** answers a different question: "what should I register, and why." That means understanding your customers, your operating model, your cash conversion cycle, your ownership chain and where you intend to be in three years, then designing a structure that supports it.

Both are legitimate services. The problem arises when someone buys the first and believes they bought the second. The structure gets chosen on price, and the consequences arrive later, usually at the bank.

## Mainland or Free Zone: The Only Test That Matters

This is the decision that determines most of what follows, and it gets made badly more often than any other.

There is no universally correct answer, and anyone who gives you one without asking about your business is selling rather than advising. What I ask instead is: **where are your customers, and how do you actually operate?**

A **mainland** licence lets you trade freely throughout the UAE and contract with government entities. If you are selling services to UAE businesses, running a clinic or a restaurant, or bidding for public sector work, this is usually where you belong.

A **free zone** can suit trading and logistics operations, holding structures, some professional services and businesses whose customers are largely outside the UAE. But "free zone" is not one thing. There are many, they differ substantially in permitted activities, costs, facilities and reputation with banks, and choosing between them properly requires knowing your activity first.

The error I see repeatedly is a business choosing a free zone for an attractive package price, then discovering its actual revenue requires trading rights it does not have. Restructuring afterwards costs more than choosing correctly would have.

## The Trade Licence Is the Beginning

A licence is permission to operate. It is not a business, and the gap between the two is where most of the real work sits.

**Corporate banking** is the step that catches people. UAE banks apply serious compliance scrutiny, and they are assessing whether your business makes sense, not just whether your documents are complete. Where do your funds come from, who ultimately owns the company, where are your customers and suppliers, and does your stated activity match what you will actually do? Applications fail on an incoherent story far more often than on a missing form. We have written separately on [how to prepare a UAE corporate bank account application](/blog/uae-corporate-bank-account-guide), and it is worth reading before you apply rather than after a rejection.

**Accounting** needs to start in month one, not at year end. This is not a compliance point, it is a management one. You cannot price properly, or know which parts of your business make money, from records assembled in a panic nine months later.

**Tax and compliance** obligations begin whether or not you owe anything. More on the numbers below.

**Operations** is the unglamorous remainder: employment contracts, visas, systems, suppliers, insurance. None of it is difficult and all of it takes longer than founders expect.

## The Numbers You Should Know Before Choosing a Structure

Structure decisions should be made against real figures, so here are the ones that matter as at September 2026.

**Corporate tax** is 0% on taxable income up to AED 375,000 and 9% above it. Registration and filing are required regardless of whether tax is payable, and the return and the payment are both due within nine months of your financial year end.

**Small Business Relief** allows a resident business with revenue of AED 3 million or less to elect to be treated as having no taxable income. This was due to expire at the end of 2026, and a great deal of published guidance still says so. It was extended by Ministerial Decision No. 131 of 2026 to tax periods ending on or before **31 December 2029**. If you built a three-year plan around the relief disappearing, that plan is worth revisiting. Note that the test is on revenue rather than profit, and the relief must be elected in a filed return rather than applying automatically.

**VAT** is 5%, with registration mandatory once taxable supplies exceed AED 375,000 over twelve months and voluntary from AED 187,500.

**Audited financial statements** are required for corporate tax purposes where revenue exceeds AED 50 million, and for all Qualifying Free Zone Persons regardless of revenue. Many free zone authorities and most banks require audited accounts independently of that, so a smaller company may still need one.

**E-invoicing** arrives on a phased timetable, with businesses at AED 50 million or above going live from January 2027 and smaller businesses from July 2027. It is a systems question rather than a filing one, which means it needs lead time.

## If You Are Coming From Outside the UAE

For an overseas founder, the paperwork is rarely the hard part. The hard part is knowing whether the market is there at all.

The questions I would want answered before we discuss a licence: is there genuine demand for this here, or is it demand you are assuming from another market? Who specifically would buy it, and do you have any evidence of that? Which emirate suits your customers, which is not always the one that suits your lifestyle? Does your model need physical premises? Should the UAE be a market for you, or a regional headquarters, because those imply different structures?

None of that is answered by a formation package. And a founder who cannot yet answer them is not ready to choose a jurisdiction, because the jurisdiction should follow the answers.

## Startups: Build Something That Can Change

Early-stage businesses face a particular tension. They need to move quickly and cheaply, but the decisions made in that first month are surprisingly difficult to reverse.

My advice is consistent: do not build the largest structure you can imagine needing. Build the smallest structure that is genuinely capable of becoming the larger one. That usually means being deliberate about the activity you register, since it constrains what you can legally do later, and honest about where your first paying customers will come from.

The sequence I find works is business model first, then jurisdiction, then licensing, then financial structure, then operations, then growth. Most founders arrive having started at licensing, and we work backwards to check the earlier decisions still hold.

## Market Entry Is Not Company Formation

A company can be perfectly incorporated and completely unable to find customers. These are separate problems and they need separate work.

Market entry means assessing whether demand exists, understanding who you are competing with and how they price, deciding how you will reach customers, and building the local relationships that in this region genuinely determine whether doors open. It is slower than incorporation and considerably more valuable.

If you are weighing the UAE against elsewhere in the region, our [Oman market entry guide](/blog/doing-business-in-oman-market-entry-guide) sets out how the same reasoning applies to a different GCC market, and why the answer is sometimes not the UAE.

## From the UAE Into the GCC

For many of the businesses we work with, Dubai or Abu Dhabi is a base rather than a destination. Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain and Oman each have their own regulators, ownership rules, customer expectations and pace.

The mistake is assuming a UAE structure automatically travels. It does not. Regional expansion needs to be sequenced, and the time to think about it is while designing the UAE entity, not two years later when the structure has to be rebuilt around a decision nobody anticipated.

## Seven Questions to Ask Any Consultant

Including us. If a consultant cannot answer these clearly, that tells you something.

1. **Which jurisdiction do you recommend, and what is the commercial reasoning?** Not "this one is popular." Why it fits your business specifically.
2. **What is the total first-year cost?** Licence, visas, premises, deposits, accounting, everything. A low headline price with costs appearing later is not a low price.
3. **What are the recurring costs?** Renewals, audit, accounting, visa cycles.
4. **What happens after incorporation?** If the relationship ends at the licence, understand that going in.
5. **Can you support the bank account?** Where the process typically stalls, and worth knowing before you commit.
6. **Can you advise on strategy, not only setup?** Relevant if you intend to grow rather than simply exist.
7. **What could go wrong with this structure?** The most revealing question of the seven. Anyone who says nothing has not thought about it.
